02 — Who Is Leaving

The Attrition Profile

The data does not describe an abstract trend. It describes a person. Here is who Watson Healthcare is losing.

The Departing Employee
Composite profile from 199 departures

The typical departing employee is an entry-level (Job Level 1) employee around 31 years old (median 29), most often a nurse (54% of leavers), with a median tenure of two years. 70% of leavers worked overtime. They earned about $4,024 a month on average ($2,741 median).

Notably, leavers' most recent raise averaged 15.2%, the same as stayers', and 53% of leavers had been promoted in the past year (38% of stayers). Pay alone does not explain departures.

03 — Key Findings

What the Data Found

Four patterns associated with attrition. These are observational findings, not proof of cause.

Finding 01 — Overtime

Employees Working Overtime Left at Nearly 6x the Rate

Employees working overtime left at 29.2%, versus 5.0% for those who did not. 70% of leavers worked overtime, against 28% of all employees. The gap appears at both pay levels checked: under $5,000 a month it is 46% vs 8%, and at $5,000 or more it is 14% vs 2%.

29% vs 5% attrition with overtime vs without
Finding 02 — Entry Level and Tenure

Entry-Level Employees in Their First Years Leave Most

Job Level 1 employees are 37% of the workforce but 71% of leavers, with 22.9% attrition versus 5.4% for everyone else. Employees with one year or less at the company left at 32%. The median leaver had two years of tenure, against six for stayers.

71% of leavers were Job Level 1 (37% of workforce)
Finding 03 — Income

Employees Under $5,000 a Month Left at About 3x the Rate

77% of leavers earned under $5,000 a month, but 51% of all employees do too, so the fairer comparison is attrition rate: 17.8% under $5,000 versus 5.6% above. Income tracks job level closely, and the gap is concentrated among Level 1 employees, where below-median pay within the level shows 30% attrition against 15% above the median. Leavers averaged $4,024 a month, against $6,852 for stayers. This is an association, not proof that pay causes exits.

17.8% vs 5.6% attrition under $5,000 a month vs $5,000 or more
Finding 04 — Work-Life Balance

Low Involvement and Poor Work-Life Balance Add Risk

Employees rating job involvement 1 or 2 (on a 4-point scale) left at 18.9%, versus 8.6% for those rating it 3 or 4. For work-life balance the split is 16.4% versus 10.1%. Employees giving the lowest work-life balance rating left at 27% (90 employees). Overtime and work-life balance are likely related, and this analysis does not separate them.

18.9% vs 8.6% attrition at low vs high job involvement
04 — Recommendations

Three Moves
for Leadership

Each recommendation ties to a finding and should be piloted and measured before a full rollout.

1

Review Entry-Level Pay and Progression

Below-median pay within Job Level 1 is associated with double the attrition. Benchmark entry-level pay against an external source first, since this dataset contains no market benchmark. Then pilot a targeted adjustment and compare 12-month retention against a control group.

Driven by findings 02 and 03
2

Build a First-Two-Years Mentor Program

First-year attrition is 32%, and low job involvement is associated with double the attrition. Pair new entry-level hires with mentors and track involvement ratings and 12-month retention against a comparison group.

Driven by findings 02 and 04
3

Test Overtime Limits and Flexible Scheduling

Overtime is the strongest signal in the data: nurses working overtime left at 32.6%, versus 5.7% without. Start with caps or rotation for sustained overtime among Level 1 employees and nurses, and measure attrition and work-life balance ratings after a pilot.

Driven by findings 01 and 04
